A Literary Map of Peru

We identify and geolocate real-world locations in Peru featured in novels by Peruvian writers. Each location is accompanied by an automated summary based on how authors describe the place and, often, how their fictional characters experience it. In the long term, we aim to include multiple authors, allowing the map to be filtered according to demographic characteristics such as gender, first language, and place of origin. The current edition focuses on novels by Mario Vargas Llosa and covers 184 unique locations and over 1,600 literary references.

PROJECT PRESENTATION AT WTC 2025

Why this project?

Beyond its recreational and academic uses, what interests me most is observing how different places are perceived and represented by different social groups, according to variables such as age, gender, place of origin, or native language. The project also makes it possible to identify which spaces are more frequently featured in Peruvian literature and which remain relatively unexplored — revealing, in the process, a Peru that escapes official state records, and helping imagine (or even reconstruct) its natural and built environments, along with the meanings and emotions attached to them over time.

Unlike other literary maps, which tend to focus on emblematic phrases, works, or authors mostly for touristic purposes, this project aims for a global collection of places. And unlike more ambitious academic projects such as A Literary Atlas of Europe, which focuses on literary analysis and on modeling narrative space in all its complexity, this one is more of an inventory and mapping of real-world references within literary texts.

Conceptual framework

A key starting point is the distinction between a map and an atlas, and the unresolved question of whether literary spaces can be translated into real spaces (a controversy associated, among others, with the work of Virginia Woolf). To classify how a place appears within a fictional text, I draw on Barbara Piatti's (2008) proposal, which distinguishes between setting, zone of action, projected space, and marker, among other categories.

Methodology: classifying places

I adapted that classification into three types, each answering a different question about the mentioned place:

1. Space of action ("setting"). Real places where the narrated action takes place — there is an event, interaction, or actual presence of characters. For example: "She lived in Lima".

2. Projected space. Places tied to a character's subjectivity — memory, desire, imagination, anticipation. The place is experienced mentally, not physically. For example: "He remembered his childhood in Arequipa".

3. Marker. Places mentioned as contextual, geographic, or narrative information, without implying direct experience or subjective weight — they serve only a descriptive or relational function. For example: "South of Lima is Ica".

In addition, each place is classified by its spatial scale — local, urban, regional, or national — with inclusion relationships that can be established between them (for example, a local place within an urban or regional unit).

Automatic place extraction

To detect places within the texts, I developed — together with Daniel — a set of linguistic rules (presented at WTC 2025) that look for two main patterns: a place indicator (such as "south of" or "in front of") followed by a proper noun, or a place noun (such as "hospital" or "plaza") followed by a proper noun. The proper noun can extend up to three additional words, allowing the system to capture cases like "La Salle School".

This first version of the code showed the kinds of errors expected from any rule-based system: one- or two-letter names misread as places, acronyms misinterpreted as locations, and words that had to be manually excluded through a banned-words list. Documenting these errors along the way has been just as important as the final result, since it shapes the direction of the next versions of the system.

Next steps: context window

The first version of the code extracted only the sentence in which a place was identified, which was enough for detection but limited for recovering information about how that place was represented in the text. For the next version, rather than relying on editorial units like the paragraph — whose length varies from book to book — I plan to extract the sentence in which the place appears along with the sentence immediately before and after it. This approach should make it possible to capture broader descriptions of space without depending on each text's formal structure.

This project is still under active development — I'll keep updating this page as the methodology and results evolve. If you'd like more technical details or want to check out the code: see it on GitHub.
